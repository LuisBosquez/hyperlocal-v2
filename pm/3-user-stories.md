# Unsolved user story bank
This list contains several stories of common situations where users aren't able to solve without our solution. These are written in first person and are meant to guide feature development for the current or future versions of this application. 

These stories are meant to be:
- Generalizable.
- Casual.
- Not too sophisticated.
- A little introspective. 

## The weather strikes again
Today, I had plans to play tennis with a new friend. When the day came, it was sunny and the energy was high. We planned to meet at Seattle U - we set a flexible time when we’ll wait until a court opens up. However, Seattle being Seattle changed her mood instantly, and the clouds and rain fluttered in unexpectedly. We had to change our plans but it is no easy feat. We can only know if the weather will be suitable a few days in advance and we need to match that with our already busy work and social schedule. We agreed to check in early next week to find a time and a place but we may or may not forget to do it.

## We should get coffee sometime.
He said the moment we were leaving from our happy hour after our soccer game. His vibe matched mine, and we have the same profession. I could always use making new friends if we can relate at a deeper level. We exchanged numbers and said we’ll check in in a few days since we both understand finding time on the spot feels a little too intense. Days went by, then months went by. Neither of us remembered. Yesterday, I got a text - he was reminded of this because of our upcoming soccer game. I agreed. He proposed a place and I reciprocated with my availability. We don’t know exactly at what time but we are both assuming it will happen.

## Real-time join 
Open invite to watch my potentially niche hobby
Today I want to watch a Euro 2024 game. I know a place that’s good and the time of the match. However, I really don’t know if my friends are into soccer - we just haven’t talked about it yet. My options are: cold-text individuals and group chats with an open invite. This isn’t ideal because I don’t know if my group chat friends might reply and also I have to spend time crafting a message and thinking about who could possibly join. 

## Last minute tickets
Seattle is weird. It’s expensive to get tickets to an event in advance but sometimes you can find sub $50 deals at the very last minute. But you don’t want to go alone. My friend took this risk last night. He bought the tickets without asking first because they were going fast and then decided to call me. I could have had any other things to do but it was worth the shot. Luckily, I didn’t and we ended up having an awesome, spontaneous time.

## I don't know what to do
Sunday mid-afternoons alone are the worst. There isn't anything intuitive to do: Food? I already ate lunch. Go out for a coffee? It's too late for more caffeine. Chores? I feel like I should be doing something with my Sunday. While I can't beat the existentialism of Sunday mid-afternoons, I wish I could just open a map and view the places I could go to near me that are suitable for the current weather, time of the day, my preferences, and maybe featuring events that are going on right now! But there isn't anything like this... yet?


## Spontaneous hanging out (Attendee edition)
### Use case
What if I wake up and I go somewhere and I signal that I’m free to hang out?

Today, I would have to think of everyone who could be interested in coming out with me and then evaluate the feasibility of them being able to come out based on their criteria, including current location, ability to travel, current plans (maybe they are working today? Or maybe they are doing something else that they mentioned to me?).

I would also like to go somewhere and check-in and potentially meet up with anyone who is there already (or close to there) and would be interested in hanging out.

I would also like to travel somewhere and automatically know who all of my friends are there and what are the places that they would recommend to go to. 

## Trip hangs: “I’m in town!” / “I’m back in town!”
We live in a globally connected society. I have friends in New York, Madrid, Paris, Mexico City (my hometown), San Diego, etc. 

Whenever I’m visiting a city that I know I have friends at, I’d love to meet up with as many of them as possible. But today, I face an issue where it’s difficult to discover who to text and how to plan. 
This is where opt-in events, announced on social media, can solve this problem. If I create an intake with multiple day options, people can sign up to hang with me whenever.
I have limited time (and bandwidth sometimes), so I’d like to be really subtle about my reaching out, but also allow enough time for planning to be convenient with other’s schedules.
This is where person-limited events can help. Every time I open the link, the availability refreshes, and it leads to plans happening without conflicts.
This is also where eventual time/space-clarification can help. It can start generically as a “afternoon in NYC”, and then, close to the date, clarify to “5pm at Central Park”.

## How do millennials and GenZ hang out?
There aren’t any specific rules, and that’s the key. Event planning tools today (partiful, facebook events, etc) assume that all the event details are finalized and specific at the time the event is created. They don’t offer many tools to make changes on the go or create an event with known unknowns. Reality, however, is different. Sometimes details of the event are to-be-defined and they start getting materialized the closer the date is to the event. In addition to this, sometimes arrival times to locations are treated as ranges as opposed to points in time.


## Time limits (AKA respond by) and guests stack
I am about to buy concert tickets but my friend has not confirmed yet. If their invite expires, I want to automatically invite someone else so I minimize the ambiguity and buy the tickets as soon as possible.
OR 
I am going to host brunch at my place, but I can only fit 6 people. I can’t easily ask everyone to respond right away and then ask the replacements if the initial invitees can’t make it. It’s also really awkward to tell someone they are a replacement.

## Recurrent events
I would like to share a booking link on my Instagram story that lets whomever book time to play tennis with me this or next week. The time availability options should be pre-defined - I can predict my weekly availability and determine that I can play on Thursdays after work. The specific times and places have not been finalized, but this can be an intake to plan in the future. 

## Subtle rejection
I sent out an invite to my friend Tim to go to the BBQ place. What I don’t know is that Tim doesn’t eat beef. Maybe he mentioned it but I honestly forgot. So when I send him the invite, he is not very keen to accept it, but he also doesn’t want to be rude. Therefore, his response will be “pass” but he will secretly include an option to organize something different to do with me in the future.

## The bail that wasn’t really a bail

It was a Wednesday. I’d joined my friend Priya’s plan to check out that new wine bar in Capitol Hill — we’d been meaning to go for weeks, we finally had a loose time, and I’d said yes. By 7pm I was completely drained. Long day, didn’t eat enough, brain fog. I texted her: “Hey, I’m so sorry, I don’t think I can make it tonight.” She was understanding, but I genuinely felt bad. Not guilty — just like I’d let something good fizzle.

The thing is, I still wanted to go. Not tonight, but soon. And I knew Priya did too. She’d been the one to save the place originally. But now we were back at square one: no plan, no momentum. I’d have to remember to bring it up the next time we talked, which meant it probably wouldn’t happen for another two months.

What I really needed in that moment wasn’t a guilt trip about cancelling. It was a quick way to say: “I still want to do this, just not right now.” Something that preserved the intent without making me commit to a new time on the spot. One tap to say “still in, eventually” and move on with my night.

## Someone just had to pick a time

My friend Marcus created a plan to go see a late-night improv show at the comedy club near Pike Place. He saved the place, marked it as a plan, and a few of us joined — me, Danny, and Sofia. It was great. Except he never added a time. Weeks went by.

The thing about Marcus is that he’s great at having ideas and terrible at following through on the logistics. He created the plan in a burst of enthusiasm and then went back to his life. The three of us who joined were obviously still interested — we’d literally clicked join — but none of us wanted to be the one to nudge him repeatedly about it. It starts to feel like you’re now organizing someone else’s plan.

Eventually Danny just texted the group chat and proposed Saturday at 8pm. Marcus said “yeah, that works.” That was the whole conversation. Why did that take a group chat and two weeks? Any of us could have put that time forward the moment we joined. Instead we all waited for the person who was least likely to act on it.

## We had the same idea all along

I’ve been wanting to go to the Japanese whisky bar in Belltown for about six months. I saved it ages ago, added a note like “for a good night with the right person.” I’d looked it up a few times, almost made a plan, never quite did.

Last week I was on my friend Nico’s profile and noticed he had it saved too. I had no idea. We’ve hung out a dozen times since I saved that place and it never came up. The overlap was just sitting there, invisible to both of us. When I saw it I laughed a little — classic case of two people independently wanting the exact same thing and never finding out.

We’ve talked about grabbing drinks a bunch of times in the abstract. But neither of us ever made it concrete, probably because neither of us wanted to feel like the one “suggesting something.” Seeing that shared save flipped something — there was nothing to negotiate. We both already knew we wanted to go.

## The place neither of us planned for

Maya and I have been mutual friends for about a year. We have plans together occasionally, usually around food — she’s adventurous, I’m always game. A few weeks ago I bailed on a plan we had at a ramen spot. I just wasn’t up for it. When I cancelled, I got the usual sheepish feeling, texted an apology, said we’d reschedule.

What I didn’t know was that we’d both independently saved a bookshop-café about ten minutes from me. I found it randomly on a Sunday afternoon, saved it, never made a plan. She’d saved it months before that after reading about it somewhere. It was the same place. We’d never talked about it.

I wouldn’t have known to suggest it. She probably wouldn’t have either. Neither of us had it in a plan — just sitting in our saved lists, waiting. If anything, the fact that we’d both found it separately, without any coordination, felt like a stronger signal than if one of us had just recommended it. It was the intersection we didn’t know we had.

That’s the kind of thing that should surface itself. Two people, same obscure place, no shared plan — someone should notice that.

## Free at 7, no plans

It was a Friday morning and for once, I had nothing planned for the evening. I opened the calendar to confirm it wasn’t a weird glitch — it wasn’t. Empty from 6pm on.

Usually that’s where it ends. I want to do something, I just don’t have the activation energy to coordinate it from scratch. Who’s free? Where? When? You ask one person and they’re not sure. You try another and it becomes a whole thread. By the time anything gets resolved it’s 9pm and the moment’s gone.

I tapped the 7pm slot and got a list of places — the wine bar in Capitol Hill near the top because, apparently, Sofia had also saved it. I didn’t know that. It had just been sitting in both our saved lists, unconnected. She appeared free based on her calendar, so the app put the two things together without me having to ask.

I picked the wine bar, she got a notification, and by noon we had a confirmed plan for that evening. Neither of us had to send a "hey, are you free tonight?" text. Neither of us had to negotiate a place from scratch. The coordination happened in maybe two minutes, most of which was me deciding I actually wanted to leave the house.

## Eight seconds of guilt

My 4pm ran until nearly 6. I had a plan with Marcus — we were going to that izakaya we’d been circling for a month. I cancelled on the way out of the conference room.

The guilt hit immediately, which is the thing about cancelling a committed plan. You said you’d go. You didn’t go. Even when it’s not your fault, there’s a small debt that forms, and the longer it stays unresolved, the more awkward it gets.

What I didn’t expect: the Google Calendar event was already gone before I’d put my phone away. And before I’d closed the app, a card appeared — "You and Marcus have a compatible idea: Izakaya Nori." One tap. Stage 1 plan, next week, no time set yet, both of us in it.

I don’t think I even fully processed what happened. One moment I was feeling like I’d dropped something, and then it was mostly handled. The debt was gone. We weren’t back at square one — we were already further along than before I cancelled, because now we both had the place in a plan instead of just saved separately.

The guilt window was about eight seconds.

## Find it by the reason I saved it

Months ago I saved Victrola, the coffee place on Capitol Hill, and I typed a little note to myself: "cortado." That’s it. That’s the whole reason. Their cortado is the thing I think about. I didn’t save it because of the name — honestly I half-forget what it’s called — I saved it because of the one thing it does that I love.

Last week a friend asked where to get a good cortado near downtown. I opened my map and typed "cortado" and got nothing useful, because the search only knows place names. The one piece of information that actually mattered — the reason I cared — was sitting right there in my own note, invisible to the search. I had to scroll through my saved places by memory until I recognized the pin.

That’s backwards. The notes I write are the most honest data in the whole app. They’re why I saved the thing. If I search "cortado," the place I noted that about should be the first result. And if my friend Nico — who I know is a coffee person — wrote the same word on some hole-in-the-wall I’ve never been to, I’d love for that to surface too. That’s the stuff Google Maps can never have, because nobody writes Google Maps a love note.

## Lists, like playlists for places

I have a Spotify playlist for basically every mood. Rainy Sunday. Pre-game hype. The one I send people when they say "what should I listen to." It costs me nothing and it says a lot about me.

I want that for places. Right now my saves are one big undifferentiated pile — the date-night wine bars are mixed in with the cheap lunch spots are mixed in with the one aquarium I saved for when my niece visits. They’re all just "saved." There’s no shape to it.

What I actually want is to make a few lists. "Capitol Hill, when you only have an hour." "Where I take out-of-town friends." "Rainy day, indoors, no agenda." Some of them I’d keep private because they’re basically my own notes. But some of them I’d absolutely make public — I’d want them on my profile, so when someone new finds me they can see my taste laid out, even before we’re friends. The same place could live in two lists and that’s fine; the wine bar is both "date night" and "Capitol Hill favorites." That’s not a contradiction, that’s just how taste works.

## Show me here, not everywhere

I opened the app the other day and the list on the side was just... a wall. My saves, my friends’ saves, plans, all stacked up, and half of them were for places nowhere near me. There was a taco spot in San Diego in there. I do not need the San Diego taco spot while I’m standing in Fremont.

It made the whole thing feel noisy, like a feed I had to fight instead of a map I could glance at. What I want is dead simple: show me a handful of good things right around where I’m looking. Not everything I’ve ever saved across three cities. Just here, just now, a short list I can actually read.

And when I do want to look somewhere else — say I’m about to head to Ballard later — I want to drag the map over there and tell it "okay, look here now," and have the list quietly become about Ballard. The map already knows where I’m looking. It should just trust that.

## Where am I, and what’s good around me right now

I was visiting a friend in a part of the city I don’t know well, killing twenty minutes before dinner. I pulled up the app on my phone and the map was centered on... somewhere else entirely, wherever I’d last left it. I just wanted one button: put me where I am. Drop a dot so I can see myself, and show me what’s worth walking to from this exact spot.

On my phone especially, panning around hunting for my own location is fiddly. I’m one-handed, I’m half-paying-attention to my friend, I just want "you are here" and three decent options nearby. That’s the whole ask. The app knows where I am if I let it — it should make it one tap to say "yes, here, now."

## Reading a friend’s map

There’s a guy I follow but haven’t really hung out with yet — we know each other from a run club. I was curious what he’s into, so I opened his profile. And the most interesting thing I could imagine seeing would be his places, on a map. Not a text list of names I don’t recognize — pins I can see, clustered in the neighborhoods he actually spends time in.

## Saturday, sometime in the afternoon

When my friend asked if I wanted to check out the new bakery this weekend, I said yes immediately — but then came the part that always stalls me out. *What time?* I genuinely didn’t know. Saturday afternoon, for sure. Two o’clock? Three? Depends on when I get up, whether I run errands first, the weather. Pinning it to an exact minute four days early felt fake, like I’d be committing to a number I’d just have to renegotiate later anyway.

What I wanted to say was exactly what I meant: “Saturday afternoon.” Not a placeholder, not “TBD” — afternoon *is* the plan. That’s real enough to put on the calendar, real enough for both of us to keep the slot open. The exact hour is a detail that wants to be filled in Saturday morning when I actually know, not a blank I’m forced to guess at now or else leave the whole thing undefined.

The nice version of this: I commit to the afternoon and move on. Then Saturday rolls around and something quietly nudges me — *want to lock in a time?* — and now, with the day in front of me, picking 2:30 takes one tap. And if I never get around to it? “Afternoon” still stands. We figure out the exact moment by text like everyone always does. The plan didn’t need the precision to be real; it just needed me to say when, roughly, and mean it.

I’d scroll around it like I’m reading a little portrait of someone. "Oh, he’s got a whole cluster in the International District." "Oh, we both saved that same dive bar." And when I tap one of his pins and it looks good, I want to just grab it — save it to my own list, or even spin it into a plan and invite him, right there. His map becomes the start of my next outing. That’s a much warmer way to get to know someone than a follower count.
